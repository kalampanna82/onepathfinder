What is USCIS premium processing? Cost, timeline, rules and benefits

Published October 23, 2026

Premium processing is a way to pay USCIS extra to look at your case within a set time. People ask about it most for student work permits (OPT), work visas such as H-1B, and employment-based green card petitions. This guide covers what it costs, how long USCIS has, the rules, and what you do and do not get for the money.

General information only, not legal advice. Fees and timeframes change, and each case is different. Check the official USCIS premium processing page and ask an immigration attorney or your school’s international student office whether it makes sense for you.

The short answer

Premium processing is an optional service. You file Form I-907 and pay a fee on top of your regular filing fee. In return, USCIS must take a first action on your case within a fixed number of business days: 15, 30 or 45, depending on the type of case. If it does not, it refunds the premium fee and keeps working on your case.

It makes the first answer come sooner. It does not make approval more likely.

What it costs

The premium fee is in addition to the regular filing fee for your form. These are the amounts in the USCIS fee schedule (Form G-1055) as of October 2026:

Most categories: H-1B, L-1, O-1, E, TN and most employment-based green card petitions (I-140)$2,965
Change of status to F, J or M (Form I-539) and certain dependents$2,075

  • USCIS can adjust these amounts for inflation every two years, so check the current schedule before you pay. Our USCIS fee calculator lists them next to the regular fees.

How long USCIS has

USCIS counts premium processing in business days, not calendar days. The federal rule (8 CFR 106.4) says a business day is a day the federal government is open. Weekends, federal holidays, and weather or other closures do not count. That means 15 business days is about three calendar weeks, and 30 business days is about six.

The rules

  • When the clock starts. For the 15-business-day cases, it starts when USCIS receives your form and the fees. For the 30 and 45 day cases, it starts when USCIS has received everything it needs to decide the case, the form and the fees included.
  • Requests for evidence (RFE) and notices of intent to deny (NOID). If USCIS sends one, the clock stops. When USCIS receives your response, a new full period begins.
  • What counts as “action.” An approval, a denial, an RFE or a NOID. USCIS does not promise an approval.
  • Refund. If USCIS does not act in time, it refunds the premium fee but keeps processing the case. There is an exception: if USCIS opens a fraud or misrepresentation investigation, it may keep the fee.
  • Not every form qualifies. The rule lists the eligible categories. Forms such as the green card application (I-485), the citizenship application (N-400) and family petitions (I-130) are not on it. For student work permits, only certain F-1 categories qualify, and USCIS announces new ones as it adds them.
  • When you can ask. You can file the I-907 with your form, or later while the case is still pending, if the category allows it.

The benefits

  • A faster first answer. Instead of waiting for the regular processing time, which can be months, you know within weeks.
  • Problems come up sooner. If USCIS needs more evidence, you hear about it earlier and can respond.
  • Planning. A fixed deadline can help with a job start date, a work permit before travel, or an H-1B start on October 1.
  • A refund if USCIS is late. You are not paying for a promise USCIS does not keep.

What it does not do

  • It does not guarantee approval or change what USCIS looks for.
  • It does not speed up card production or mail. After an approval, a work permit card can take another one to three weeks to arrive.
  • It does not make a green card available sooner. An I-140 approval does not move your place in the line. Your priority date still has to be current in the Visa Bulletin.
  • It does not speed up other steps in your process, such as a visa interview or a background check.

Is it worth it?

That depends on your situation. These are the questions people usually weigh:

  1. How long is regular processing for your form right now? Check USCIS case processing times. If the regular time is already short, you may not gain much.
  2. Do you have a hard date, such as a job start, a travel plan or a status that ends soon?
  3. Can you afford the fee, and is your case ready? A case with missing documents may draw an RFE either way, and the fee does not fix that.
  4. Is your employer paying? For many work visas the employer files and pays.

An immigration attorney or your school’s international student office can tell you what fits your case.

How to request it

  1. Check that your form and category are eligible on the USCIS premium processing page.
  2. File Form I-907 with your case, or on its own for a pending case, along with the premium fee. Pay it separately from the regular filing fee. Use the current form edition and follow the form instructions.
  3. Keep your receipt notice. It shows the date USCIS received your request, which USCIS uses to start the count.
  4. Check your case status online, and answer any RFE or NOID quickly.

Common mistakes

  • Counting calendar days instead of business days.
  • Expecting an approval, when the promise is an action.
  • Forgetting the card still has to be made and mailed.
  • Paying an old fee amount, which can get the request rejected.
  • Using premium processing on a case that is not ready, which can lead to an RFE.
