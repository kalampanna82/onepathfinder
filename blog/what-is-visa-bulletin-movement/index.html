What is Visa Bulletin movement?

Published October 22, 2026

When people in green card lines talk about the Visa Bulletin, they often say a date “moved” or “retrogressed.” That is Visa Bulletin movement. This guide explains what it is, what each kind of movement means, and what it does not mean. It helps to know what the Visa Bulletin and a priority date are first.

General information only, not legal advice. Movement tells you what happened to a cutoff date. It does not tell you what will happen to your case. Please check the current bulletin on the official site and talk to a licensed immigration attorney or a DOJ-accredited representative about your situation.

The short answer

Visa Bulletin movement is how much a cutoff date in the bulletin changed from one month to the next, for one category and one country. You compare this month’s date with last month’s. The change is the movement.

The kinds of movement

  • Forward. The cutoff date moves to a later date. More people qualify, because everyone whose priority date is earlier than the new date can go ahead.
  • No movement. The date stays the same. Sometimes called “holding.” Nobody new qualifies that month.
  • Backward (retrogression). The cutoff date moves to an earlier date. Fewer people qualify than the month before.
  • Becoming current or unavailable. A date can change to C (current: every priority date qualifies) or to U (unavailable: no visas that month). Going from C to a date counts as retrogression. Going from U to a date or to C is forward movement.

A worked example

In the November 2026 bulletin, the Final Action Date for the second employment-based category (EB-2) for most countries moved from January 1, 2025 to April 1, 2025. That is forward movement of three months. If your EB-2 priority date is February 15, 2025, it was not earlier than the old date but it is earlier than the new one. In November, a visa number is available for your priority date in that category.

The same bulletin moved the family category F1 for most countries from January 22, 2020 to January 22, 2021. That is a one-year jump in a single month. Compare it with EB-2 for most countries, which was “current” in the August 2026 bulletin and now has a cutoff date. That is retrogression, even though the date itself looks recent.

What movement means for you

  • Forward movement means the line is moving. If your priority date is now earlier than the cutoff date, a visa number is available for you that month. That is not an approval. Your case still has to be filed or reviewed and approved.
  • No movement means you keep waiting. It does not mean anything is wrong with your case.
  • Retrogression usually means people in line may have to wait longer. You keep your priority date and your place in line. Retrogression does not cancel a petition or erase your priority date. What it can do is stop final approval until your date is earlier than the cutoff date again.

Why dates move

The law sets yearly limits on family-sponsored and employment-based visas, and a per-country limit of 7 percent of the total. The State Department matches the visa numbers available against the demand it knows about, in priority date order. When demand in a category is lower than the numbers available, the date can advance quickly. When demand is higher, the date holds or moves backward. Each fiscal year starts on October 1 with a new set of numbers, which is why October and November bulletins can show large changes. The October 2026 bulletin itself says some employment-based categories for most countries were pulled back to keep issuances within the yearly and quarterly limits.

What movement does not tell you

  • It does not predict the future. A date that jumped a year this month can hold, or retrogress, next month. Nobody can reliably say how long your wait will be.
  • A big jump is not a shorter wait by the same amount. If a date moves forward one year, that does not mean you are one year closer on your own timeline.
  • It does not say which chart you can use. Movement can be different in the Final Action Dates chart and the Dates for Filing chart. For a green card application inside the United States, USCIS says each month which chart to use. Check the USCIS Visa Bulletin page.

Looking at movement over 1, 3 and 6 months

One month can be noisy. Comparing this month with 3 or 6 months ago shows the bigger trend: a category that is steadily advancing, one that is stuck, or one that went backward. Remember that this only compares two bulletins, so a date that moved up and then back down in between shows up as the net change.

What to do with this

  1. Find your priority date, your category and your country.
  2. Check whether it is earlier than the cutoff date in the newest bulletin, or use the priority date checker for family categories.
  3. Look at the movement for your category to see whether the line is advancing, stuck or going backward.
  4. Before you file or change anything because of movement, confirm the chart USCIS allows that month and ask a licensed attorney or accredited representative.
